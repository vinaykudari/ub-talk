A real challenge

Fix one thing.
Keep everything else working.

Find recurring failures. Improve the harness without breaking other tasks.

Slide 6 · Fix one thing, keep everything else working · ~1.5 min

A practical challenge is finding recurring failure or loss patterns across a huge and varied set of real user tasks. I'm using those words to describe weaknesses in task behavior, not a specific model-training loss function.

Once we identify a weakness, improving the harness must also account for its other behavior. A change aimed at one capability can conflict with another task or regress something that already works. The illustration shows the goal: address the recurring weakness while preserving behavior across other tasks, rather than claiming those outcomes have already been measured.

Scale makes both parts harder: spotting useful patterns in a large volume of feedback, and understanding the effect of a change across diverse GCP usage. This is a conceptual description of the challenge, not a particular evaluation algorithm, customer-data pipeline, or deployment process.

Slide 7 · Will AI Take Our Jobs? · ~2 min

When you're getting ready to enter the workforce, “Will AI take our jobs?” is a reasonable question. And that worry has a history. In 1930, a newspaper letter warned that electric power would put thousands out of employment. In the 1980s, teachers protesting calculators worried about students losing basic skills. In 1995, reporting on the internet captured fears that it would probably destroy jobs. Those concerns weren't all the same, and we shouldn't dismiss them as all wrong.

I see technological change as a kind of evolution. The transition can be disruptive, uncertain, even chaotic, and people have to adapt as things change. But that doesn't tell us how this particular transition will turn out. AI reaches into a broad range of cognitive tasks, and its capabilities are changing quickly. So let's look at what makes this wave different.

Slide 8 · From “How do I do it?” to “Do it for me” · ~2 min

The last slide showed that worries about technology have a history. So why does this wave feel different? AI reaches across a broad range of cognitive tasks, and it can use tools to act. Those capabilities are moving quickly. That opens up more problems you can build solutions for.

Look at this six-month stretch. In April, Meta showed AI helping plan a family trip: compare destinations, draft an itinerary, find activities. In September, it introduced Muse as an agent that could book travel and continue working after the app closed. That's a change in the kind of help you can hand over.

By today, Google's Gemini documentation describes calling businesses to book reservations, navigate phone menus, and wait on hold. Claude's October rollout puts new Cowork tasks and recurring work in the cloud on Pro and Max. Think about the possibilities: a booking assistant, a reservation desk, or a daily research scout for your lab.

And AI helps with the building itself: writing a first prototype, exploring an idea, and getting to something you can test faster. Meta's April examples already included creating websites from a prompt. So there's movement on both sides: what your solution can do, and how quickly you can start building it.

I'm not saying these problems were impossible throughout history. Something that was too slow, costly, or difficult for one student may now be practical to try. Find a problem you care about, and ask whether the new capability makes a useful solution possible. Progress doesn't mean every agent works reliably. That's why, on the next slide, I want to talk about both the potential and the remaining gap.

Evidence and limits: these are selected product changes, not universal invention dates or a benchmark comparison. April's Meta AI already used multiple agents. Muse is an agent product; Muse Spark is a model. Gemini calling is a gradual rollout for eligible US adults using Pixel 11, a Google AI subscription, English, and the Phone app public beta; task details are reviewed and users can monitor or take over. October 6 expanded cloud Cowork for Pro and Max; cloud beta began July 7. Old local tasks remain local; local resources can need the desktop app. An ongoing goal can resume on schedules or events without continuous model inference. The three builder ideas are conceptual opportunities, not promises of consumer-product APIs or successful autonomous outcomes.

Slide 9 · My own swing · ~2 min

Before the talk: re-check the RLI leaderboard number (21.25% as of Oct 9, 2026).

Let me tell you about my own swing on this, because I go back and forth.

When I use a frontier model like Opus 4.6 for coding, there are moments when I genuinely think: is software development over? It writes things in minutes that would take me hours.

Then I go back to my day job, building agents, and I see the other side: they're still far from replacing humans. They get stuck, they make subtle mistakes, and they can confidently do the wrong thing.

Here's data that captures both feelings. Scale AI and the Center for AI Safety built the Remote Labor Index: 240 real, paid freelance projects from Upwork, averaging almost 29 hours of human work each. At launch, the best agent completed 2.5% of them at a quality a client would accept. Today the top of the leaderboard is about 21%. So even the best agent still fails about four out of five real jobs. But it went from 2.5 to 21. We're getting closer.

Andrej Karpathy put it well in January: if you have any code you actually care about, watch the models "like a hawk." Both things are true at once, and holding both in your head is the right mindset.

Slide 10 · What's coming · ~2 min

So what's coming? Three things I'm fairly confident about.

First, smaller teams. When each person has agents doing parts of the work, you need fewer people to ship something real. Paul Graham described a startup with six employees that told him they'd need about ten more people without AI.

Second, more startups. Base44 is my favorite example. A solo founder, Maor Shlomo, built a vibe-coding platform, and Wix acquired it about six months after he founded it, for roughly $80 million up front plus earn-outs. The company had about six employees and no outside funding. That kind of outcome used to take a big team and venture money.

Third, and this is the one I care about most: bigger, more complex problems get solved. When the cost of building drops, problems that were never worth a team's time suddenly become worth solving. Things get cheaper and faster, and the world starts to run more efficiently.

For you, this means the menu after graduation is bigger than "apply to a big company." You can join a tiny team, start one, or take on a problem nobody has bothered to fix. Start with a problem that makes you curious.

Slide 11 · Fundamentals + AI · ~2.5 min

So how do you prepare? I would focus on four habits that work together.

First, master the fundamentals. Data structures, systems, math, networks, databases: learn why things work, not just the syntax. That understanding helps you choose an approach and recognize when an AI answer is plausible but wrong. Simon Willison makes this point in his account of AI-assisted coding: existing expertise helps you direct the tool, and you still have to test what it produces.

Second, learn actively with AI. Use it to explain an unfamiliar concept, ask you questions, and critique your reasoning. Then try the problem yourself and explain the solution back. Finishing a task is not the same as learning it. A small study of developers learning a new library found that how people used AI mattered: the stronger learners asked conceptual questions and worked to understand the generated code. Treat that as a useful learning habit, not a promise that one technique always works.

Third, prompt with precision. Learn the language of your field so you can describe the problem accurately. Knowing what idempotency means, for example, helps you ask for retries that won't duplicate an order. The useful part is understanding the requirement; the technical term gives it a name. Give the AI the goal, relevant context, constraints, and what a good result looks like. If you don't know the terminology yet, ask it to help identify the concept, then check it against documentation. This is a communication skill you develop through practice and feedback.

Fourth, build tools you actually use. Start with a recurring task in your own work: processing lab data, organizing research, or checking a result. Use AI to help build a small tool, try it on real inputs, and improve it when it fails. That gives you practice directing AI and a concrete way to judge whether it helped. Fundamentals, active learning, precise instructions, and useful tools reinforce each other.

Let's make this concrete on a résumé. “Built an AI assistant with tool calling and memory” names capabilities, but doesn't tell me what you personally solved or how anyone checked the work. AI makes it easier to generate a demo or adapt an existing project. The stronger signal is a useful change you can explain and point to.

One route I would actively encourage is contributing to open source. Pick an agent project you actually use, like OpenClaw or Hermes Agent from Nous Research. Find a real issue, understand the existing code, make a focused change, test it, and respond to review. Getting a meaningful contribution accepted into an established project adds outside validation: you're meeting someone else's requirements and working through their feedback. That can be more informative than a standalone demo. OpenClaw explicitly welcomes AI-assisted contributions, with the same quality and review standards. Using AI doesn't remove your responsibility to understand the change.

The stronger résumé example imagines fixing duplicate agent replies in OpenClaw, adding a regression test, and getting the fix merged after review. It's an illustrative bullet, not a claim about a specific contributor or a verified pull request. In your own résumé, name the actual issue you solved and link your accepted contribution. Be ready to walk through the failure, the fix, and what reviewers asked you to change. A cloned repository is a useful starting point for learning; be clear about the work you added and credit the project you built on.

The second example is an agent for your parents: it compares repair quotes, identifies useful differences, and drafts follow-up questions for approval. The outcome is replacing manual quote tracking with a repeatable workflow. Now the project has a reason to exist, real users, and actions you can demonstrate. Explain what the agent does, where the person stays involved, and whether anyone actually uses it. Include measured time savings only if you have the measurements. A small project used by your family can be a strong story too.

One thing I don't want you handing entirely to AI is your résumé. Use its assistance: ask what is unclear, what detail is missing, or how to shorten a sentence. Then rewrite it in language you would actually use. I'd rather read an honest, slightly awkward sentence you can explain than a polished paragraph that says nothing about you. An occasional spelling or grammar mistake matters less to me than whether the work is yours. But still proofread: authentic doesn't have to mean careless, and other reviewers may judge those errors differently.

Harvard's career guidance puts it directly: “Generative AI should not be the primary author.” The University of Michigan says, “Edit the bullets so that they are accurate and written in your own voice.” That's the balance. Start with your facts, use AI for feedback, and own the final wording. Keep relevant technical terms, but don't borrow claims or invented numbers. Read every line aloud and ask yourself: could I explain exactly how I did this? The next slide is about making that work easy for someone else to check.

Slide 13 · Build your public profile · ~3 min

That brings me to a bigger point: your résumé is not your whole profile. A résumé is a snapshot of what you say you've done. A résumé bullet is easy to invent. I put more trust in a history that connects your posts, projects, feedback, and the people you worked with. Those connections give someone more ways to check what you actually did. So start building that history now.

In the agentic era, making a polished piece of content is easier. What makes your content useful is the firsthand experience behind it: your decisions, your experiments, your mistakes, and what changed when you tried again. Make original posts about something you actually learned or built. Instead of reposting an announcement, explain what happened when you used the tool. What worked? What failed? What would you do differently?

Then go deeper in articles and blog posts. Write the explanation you wish you'd had when you started. Show the example, the result, the limitation, and the link to the work. You don't need to be an expert before you write. You can say, “Here's what I tried, and here's what I still don't understand.” AI can help edit or organize it, but the observations and judgment should come from you.

Make demos. Go to hackathons. Publish a short walkthrough of what your team built and be clear about your own part. Then write the follow-up: what did you improve after someone used it? A working demo, an accepted pull request, and a thoughtful account of the tradeoffs tell a richer story together than any one of them alone.

I've seen this happen in my own career. A while back, I took part in a DeepMind hackathon in San Francisco and made it to the finals. There was a recruiter at the event, and I was contacted afterward. That connection became part of the path that brought me to Google. What made that opportunity possible was having something to show: people could see the work, rather than just read about it on a résumé.

And meet people around the work. Talk to other builders, ask a useful question, share your write-up, help with their project, and follow up. Networking becomes more natural when there's something specific to talk about. Over time, people have seen how you think, how you build, and how you respond to feedback.

That's what I mean by building a profile. Your résumé can point to it, but a few polished sentences can't replace it. This is not a contest to post every day or collect followers. Create something useful, share what you learned, and keep going. Let your body of work grow alongside your skills.
