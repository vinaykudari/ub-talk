Building in the

AGI Era

Vinay Kudari

Slide 1 · Title · ~30 sec

Until yesterday, this talk was called "Building in the Age of Agents." This morning, I updated it to "Building in the AGI Era." I figured if we're going to talk about building for the future, we should start looking a little further ahead.

I'll start with a little about my journey from UB to the work I do today, and what building agents has taught me along the way. Then I want to talk about where AI might be heading, how that changes the problems we can take on, and how you can prepare as you start your own careers. Let's get into it.

Slide 2 · From 2022 to 2026 · ~3 min

A quick bit on how I got here. I graduated from UB with my M.S. in Computer Science in 2022, and started at Amazon, working on data pipelines and backend systems.

Then I moved to Gap, expecting to work on machine learning systems. The recommendation systems I ended up working on weren't ML-based, so it wasn't quite the move I had imagined. But I kept looking for opportunities through side projects and hackathons at work. Eventually, I was able to pivot into ML. That was my first transition into machine learning within a company.

After that, I moved to Apple, where most of my work was on agentic systems for finance. Those were mainly internal projects, but the experience opened a path to my current role at Google as an agentic engineer.

A significant part of my job now is research, which is something I'd always wanted to do. It took about four years of being intentional about the work I pursued to get here. My coursework, projects, and time working with professors at UB all played a big part in that journey.

So if I could pause here and give one piece of advice: don't choose your courses just because they're easy and leave you more time for interview prep. Preparing for interviews matters, but this is also your chance to explore. If you haven't found what interests you yet, take courses that help you figure that out. And if you already know what interests you, go deep. The understanding and experience you build now can shape opportunities well beyond your first job.

Slide 3 · What I work on · ~1 min

So what do I actually work on? I work on a harness that helps Gemini operate Google Cloud.

Think of Google Cloud as a huge, complex computer shared by lots of people at the same time. Each person or team has services running on it that need to be managed and maintained. The harness gives Gemini a way to use tools and help with that work on your behalf.

My specific project is recursive self-improvement, or RSI. I conduct research on how to model an agent's behavior, learn from its mistakes, and build a self-improving loop that improves the harness and improves Gemini through post-training.

Project description: Vinay's account of his team's work and his project.

The goal

A better harness. A better Gemini.

Slide 4 · Two improvement paths · ~1 min

A harness is the software around the model that lets it get work done. Gemini provides the reasoning. The harness gives it the tools and context to act, keeps track of what's happened, and controls what it's allowed to do.

Say a cloud service stops responding. The model might decide to check the logs. The harness runs that tool and brings the results back to the model, which can then decide what to try next. That cycle continues until the task is done, or the agent needs help or approval.

When the agent works on a task, we can learn from what it does and how it turns out. That feedback can help us improve the harness and, through post-training, improve Gemini too. Here's how I think about that improvement loop.

Recursive self-improvement

Real use drives a better harness

Slide 5 · Real-use-driven harness improvement · ~2 min

When an agent attempts a task, I want to understand its behavior: what actions it took, what happened, and where it went wrong or could have done better. Looking across those attempts helps us identify patterns rather than treating each mistake in isolation.

We can use what we learn to improve the harness, then see how it performs on future tasks. Those attempts reveal new things to work on, and the cycle continues. That's the self-improving loop I'm working toward.

Post-training means giving an already-trained model more focused training so it gets better at particular tasks. Useful examples could include a task, the tools available, the actions taken, the results those tools returned, and a checked outcome. A good demonstration shows how to do the task; a failed attempt helps us find situations the model needs more practice with.

Think back to how you might have trained your first reinforcement learning model. You let an agent explore an environment, try different actions, and receive rewards for making progress or completing the task. Training makes the behavior that earns those rewards more likely. It's the same basic idea here, except the actions involve using tools to work on cloud infrastructure. For example, did it actually restore a service and pass the checks, while respecting the constraints? That's the kind of result we want to reward, rather than just an answer that sounds convincing.

A real challenge

Fix one thing.
Keep everything else working.

Find recurring failures. Improve the harness without breaking other tasks.

Slide 6 · Fix one thing, keep everything else working · ~2 min

A practical challenge is finding recurring failure or loss patterns in the agent's actions. Agents aren't deterministic: the same request can lead to different sequences of actions, and there are often several valid ways to solve a problem. So we can't judge every run against one exact sequence of steps.

By a loss pattern, I mean a recurring weakness in how the agent tackles a task. It doesn't have to fail outright. It might finish, but keep making unnecessary tool calls, retrying something that won't work, or making a broad change when a smaller one would have been enough. We're looking at how it got to the result, as well as whether it got there.

How do we make this debugging superhuman? How can we spot a shared weakness across thousands of very different attempts, understand what's causing it, and improve the harness without breaking something else that already works? That's a big part of the challenge.

And privacy and security have to be part of that from the start. These systems may have access to sensitive data and the ability to change live infrastructure. A small mistake, like changing the wrong configuration, could take a service offline. Think about the stakes for a service at Spotify's scale; Spotify uses Google Cloud. An outage can interrupt people's access, lose business, and damage trust. Every minute matters. The agent needs to respect permissions, protect data, and know when to ask for approval.

Slide 7 · Will AI Take Our Jobs? · ~2 min

Whenever a big new technology comes along, it creates headlines and questions about the future. AI is no different. A question many of us have is: will AI take our jobs? Let's look at how people reacted to some of the big changes that came before.

In 1930, a newspaper letter warned that electric power would put thousands out of work. In 1945, the Los Angeles Times called the atomic bomb “man's most destructive force.” That's a different concern from jobs. The danger was real, and nuclear weapons are still dangerous. At the same time, having them can help prevent attacks and keep people safe. And in 1995, a news report said the internet would probably destroy jobs. These were very different technologies, and the concerns weren't all the same or all wrong. Each made people think about how a powerful new technology could change the world.

I think AI could be an even bigger shift than the internet. The internet connected people around the world and opened up access to knowledge, services, and markets. Now we're adding intelligence that can work across those connections: understand a problem, find information, use tools, and take action. We built a connected world. Now we're building systems that can get things done in it.

Slide 8 · METR time horizons · ~2 min

So how quickly is this changing? METR, a research organization that evaluates AI systems, measures what it calls the time horizon. It asks: how long would a task take a human expert when an AI agent is predicted to succeed about half the time?

The examples on the left give you a feel for those task lengths, from finding a fact to building a simple web server or training a classifier. These are mostly well-defined software tasks, and the vertical axis uses a logarithmic scale. The examples aren't a guarantee that an agent can do every task at that level.

The solid line shows selected estimates through early 2026, rising from a few minutes for GPT-4 to roughly twelve hours for Opus 4.6. The estimates are uncertain, especially near the top. The dashed line asks what might happen if the historical trend continued. It's a thought experiment, not a prediction; METR says its current suite becomes unreliable above sixteen hours.

What does that mean if you're choosing a project today? If models can do more six months or a year from now, what will make what you're building valuable? We'll come back to that. First, let me share how working with these systems changed my own thinking.

Slide 9 · What changed my mind · ~2 min

At Apple, I was working on agentic systems for finance. When I started using Opus 4.6, it handled so much of the implementation that I barely had to stop and think or research things. For the problems I was working on then, it felt like most of the coding was becoming automatic. I remember thinking: is software development over?

Then I moved to Google and started working on much harder problems. Even with the latest models, I was back to spending a lot of time thinking, researching, trying different approaches, and figuring out why something wasn't working. That's when I realized how far agents still have to go. The models hadn't become less capable. The problems I was asking them to help solve had become harder.

And I think that's what we'll keep seeing. As AI makes today's work easier, we'll take on problems that used to be out of reach. Things we wouldn't have attempted with the time, people, or tools we had before. Our idea of what's worth trying will keep growing.

So I don't look at my experience at Apple and conclude that software development is over. I look at what I'm working on now and see how much more there is to build. More of the coding will be automated, but that lets us tackle harder problems. That's where I think our work is going.

Source: Vinay's firsthand experience at Apple and Google. The outlook on harder problems is his personal perspective.

Slide 10 · What's coming · ~2 min

So what's coming? Here are three things I'm fairly confident about.

First, smaller teams. As agents take on more work, small teams may be able to build things that used to need more people. Paul Graham shared an example of a six-person startup using AI. The team estimated that without AI, they'd need around sixteen people to do the same work.

Second, more startups. My favorite example is Base44. A solo founder, Maor Shlomo, built a vibe-coding platform. About six months after he started it, Wix bought it for roughly $80 million up front, plus earn-outs. The company had about six employees and no outside funding. That used to take a big team and venture money.

Third, and this is the one I care about most: we get to solve bigger, harder problems. When building gets cheaper, problems that were never worth a team's time suddenly are. That gives us more opportunities to make everyday experiences better.

For you, that means life after graduation isn't just "apply to a big company." You can join a tiny team, start one, or go after a problem nobody's bothered to fix. Start with a problem that makes you curious.

Develop trust in AI.

Slide 11 · Develop trust in AI · ~2 min

I want to share four ideas on how we can prepare for what's coming. The first is to develop trust in AI. I think these systems will keep getting better. I don't think we can simply stop that progress, so I want us to put our energy into understanding the risks and making the technology safer.

There are real reasons for concern: mistakes, privacy, misuse, and what happens to people's work. We should take those seriously. At the same time, when I look at what AI is making possible, I see a lot of reasons to be hopeful.

It's helping researchers understand genetic changes, helping doctors find signs of cancer, and helping scientists explore new drug candidates. Researchers are also studying how it might help predict patient outcomes. Those are examples where better tools could make a real difference to people's lives. They still need careful testing, and experts have to be involved.

And as exciting as that is, I think the transition is going to ask a lot of us.

Prepare for the chaos.

Slide 12 · Prepare for the chaos · ~1.5 min

I think the world we're entering could look very different from the one we're used to. New tools will change how we work, what small teams can do, and the kinds of skills people need. I don't know exactly how that transition will unfold.

There may be a period of real chaos, and some people could go through a lot of struggle. I don't want to pretend that everyone will experience this change in the same way, or that adapting will be easy.

But periods of upheaval can also push us to learn, adapt, and find new ways forward. So stay curious. Keep your fundamentals strong. Learn to use these tools, and keep looking for hard problems worth solving. The goal isn't to have the whole future figured out; it's to be ready to keep learning as it unfolds.

And that brings me to a question I've been thinking about a lot: with models improving so quickly, what should we choose to build?

Outlook on disruption and adaptation is Vinay's perspective. OECD Employment Outlook 2026 discusses varying and uncertain labor-market effects.

Build for the future.

Slide 13 · Build for the future · ~2 min

Remember the question from the METR chart: as models get better, what will make your project valuable? Here's how I think about it. If models get ten times better, is there still a hard and useful problem here?

I want better models to make my project more powerful. A simple prototype is a great way to learn, but if the whole project is just getting today's model to do something it struggles with, tomorrow's model might make that part much easier. Think about what you can add around it.

That could be a real need you've understood by talking to people, data that needs to be collected, experiments in the world, or a system that has to work reliably. Science, healthcare, logistics, and robotics all have problems like that. And I believe robotics will become much more mainstream as the models behind it improve. There will still be a lot of hard work in sensing, acting, testing, and making things useful in the physical world.

Even if we get to AGI, the question doesn't disappear: what do we want to do with it? What problems are worth solving next? Start with something that matters to someone, then build in a way that gets stronger as the tools improve.

The ten-times-better scenario, robotics outlook, project-selection questions, and view of AGI are Vinay's thought experiment and perspective, not predictions of a specific timeline or model capability. Examples of domains are starting points rather than guarantees that every project in them remains difficult.

Build your public profile.

Slide 14 · Build your public profile · ~3 min

Once you start working on problems you care about, let people see the work. Your résumé is a snapshot. It's easy to make a claim on a page, but a history of projects, writing, and contributions gives people more ways to see what you've actually done. I think that will matter more as hiring changes.

Contribute to open source. Find a project you use, understand an issue, and make a change you can explain. When a contribution is accepted, you've worked with someone else's code and met their bar. That's something you can show, and the conversation around that work is part of your story too.

Share your ideas, experiments, and the things you learn. Write a post or a blog about what you tried, what worked, and what didn't. You don't have to be an expert. The value is in your own experience and the way you think through it.

For me, writing is part of thinking. So I usually don't ask AI to create my first draft. I write it myself, even if it's messy, and then use AI to challenge or improve it. Joining a writing club has helped me practice that. As the tools get better, I don't want to lose the ability to form my own thoughts.

The same goes for your résumé: don't let AI write it for you. Use it for feedback, but make sure the words are yours and you can explain every line. I think more screening will look beyond a school name or a project title and toward the fuller picture of what you've made and shared.

And go to hackathons. I took part in Zero to Agent, a Vercel and DeepMind hackathon in San Francisco, and our project Duckie made it to the finals. A recruiter was there and reached out afterward. That connection became part of my path to Google. People had a chance to see the work and talk to me about it. That's the kind of opportunity you create when you put your work out there.

Slide 15 · People and perspective · ~2 min

That hackathon experience also reminded me how much the people around you can matter. Living in Silicon Valley has been an important part of my journey. I've met people who are building and thinking about the future, and being around them has changed how I think and what I believe is possible.

If you get the opportunity to move there, I'd encourage you to consider it. And if that's not possible, you can still learn from people you admire wherever you are. Follow their work, read their blogs, listen to their podcasts, and find people you can learn and build with.

Following people who think about these questions has also given me a sense of how much change may be ahead. Eric Schmidt, a former Google CEO, has spoken about how profoundly AI could change the world. In 2025, he suggested that superintelligence could arrive within about six years. That's a prediction, and nobody knows the timeline. But it captures why I think this is such an important moment.

I think we may have only a few more years when humans are at the frontier of so much intellectual work. So I want to make the most of this time, and I'd encourage you to do the same. Be productive, keep learning, and put your energy into problems that matter to you. As these tools get smarter, we can use them to do work that matters and contribute in new ways.
