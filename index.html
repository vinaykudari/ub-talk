The loop

Agents that make agents better

Real work in, a better model out, and around again.

Slide 4 · The loop · ~2 min

CONFIRM this high-level framing is OK to share publicly.

Here's the part I find most exciting, and I'll keep it high-level. Think of it as a loop.

At the top, an agent harness runs real tasks: real work, in real environments. Every run produces trajectories, meaning the steps the agent took, what worked and what didn't. That's data. That data helps improve Gemini; the learnings get upstreamed to Google DeepMind. A better model makes a better agent. And the better agent goes back into the harness and takes on harder tasks. Then the loop repeats.

People call this recursive self-improvement, or RSI. I want to be careful here: this isn't a magic machine that improves itself overnight. Every arrow in this loop has humans in it. People design the tasks, build the evaluations, decide what "good" looks like, and catch the failures.

But the shape matters. It's the same shape as that RL class: act, get feedback, improve. Except now the "agent" is a production system and the "environment" is real work. And how fast each turn of the loop goes is a big part of why this era feels so different.

Slide 6 · "This will kill us" · ~2 min

Whenever a big technology shows up, someone says: this will kill us. Sometimes literally.

In 1888, during the war of the currents, Harold P. Brown wrote to the New York Evening Post that among electric lighting men, alternating current was "appropriately called 'undertaker's wire.'" Today it's in the walls of the room you're sitting in.

In 1986, math teachers picketed outside the national math teachers' convention over calculators in elementary classrooms. One sign read: "The Button's Nothin' 'Til the Brain's Trained." And honestly? That sign was half right. I'll come back to it.

In 1995, Clifford Stoll wrote in Newsweek, under the print headline "The Internet? Bah!", that "no online database will replace your daily newspaper."

And in 2017, The Atlantic ran a cover story asking, "Have Smartphones Destroyed a Generation?" To be fair, the debate about phones and mental health is still very much live. I'm presenting it as a fear from its time, not as a debunked one.

The pattern: each time, the fear had a kernel of truth, the technology spread anyway, and the people who learned to use it well came out ahead. So when you hear "AI will kill us," the honest question is: is this time different?

Sources: Zócalo Public Square (Brown's 1888 letter) · LA Times, May 17, 1986 · Washington Post, Apr 1986 · Newsweek, Stoll 1995 · The Atlantic, Twenge 2017

Slide 7 · But this time is different · ~2 min

And here's my honest answer: in some important ways, yes, this time is different.

Speed. According to the Stanford AI Index 2026, generative AI reached 53% adoption in three years, which is faster than the personal computer or the internet.

Scale. ChatGPT alone now has 1.2 billion weekly users, according to OpenAI, as reported by The Verge at the end of September.

Capability. METR measured that the length of tasks AI agents can complete on their own, at 50% reliability, has been doubling roughly every seven months for six years. A fair caveat: those are self-contained, well-specified tasks, and METR itself says that doesn't mean jobs are automated. But the trend line is steep.

The same AI Index chapter reports that employment for software developers aged 22 to 25 has fallen nearly 20% since 2024. I'm not saying that to scare you. I'm saying it because the impact is different in kind, and that changes the strategy. In past waves you could wait and pick things up later. This one moves too fast for that. The people who learn intensively now, not someday, get to ride the curve instead of chasing it.

Slide 8 · My own swing · ~2 min

Before the talk: re-check the RLI leaderboard number (21.25% as of Oct 9, 2026).

Let me tell you about my own swing on this, because I go back and forth.

When I use a frontier model like Opus 4.6 for coding, there are moments when I genuinely think: is software development over? It writes things in minutes that would take me hours.

Then I go back to my day job, building agents, and I see the other side: they're still far from replacing humans. They get stuck, they make subtle mistakes, and they can confidently do the wrong thing.

Here's data that captures both feelings. Scale AI and the Center for AI Safety built the Remote Labor Index: 240 real, paid freelance projects from Upwork, averaging almost 29 hours of human work each. At launch, the best agent completed 2.5% of them at a quality a client would accept. Today the top of the leaderboard is about 21%. So even the best agent still fails about four out of five real jobs. But it went from 2.5 to 21. We're getting closer.

Andrej Karpathy put it well in January: if you have any code you actually care about, watch the models "like a hawk." Both things are true at once, and holding both in your head is the right mindset.

Slide 9 · What's coming · ~2 min

So what's coming? Three things I'm fairly confident about.

First, smaller teams. When each person has agents doing parts of the work, you need fewer people to ship something real. Paul Graham described a startup with six employees that told him they'd need about ten more people without AI.

Second, more startups. Base44 is my favorite example. A solo founder, Maor Shlomo, built a vibe-coding platform, and Wix acquired it about six months after he founded it, for roughly $80 million up front plus earn-outs. The company had about six employees and no outside funding. That kind of outcome used to take a big team and venture money.

Third, and this is the one I care about most: bigger, more complex problems get solved. When the cost of building drops, problems that were never worth a team's time suddenly become worth solving. Things get cheaper and faster, and the world starts to run more efficiently.

For you, this means the menu after graduation is bigger than "apply to a big company." You can join a tiny team, start one, or take on a problem nobody has bothered to fix. Let me show you one.

Slide 10 · A problem you can solve · ~2.5 min

Let me give you an example of a problem like that, from my own life. Recently, I witnessed an accident in San Francisco. A truck driver's blind spot hid someone. The driver simply couldn't see them. [Your story, in your words.]

It stuck with me, so I looked into it. According to the FMCSA, one-third of all crashes between large trucks and cars take place in the "No-Zone," the truck's blind spots. Their page says some of these blind spots are large enough that a passenger vehicle can "virtually disappear" from the driver's view. And IIHS reports that 5,340 people died in large-truck crashes in the US in 2024.

Here's the hopeful part: this is a vision problem, and AI vision can see what drivers can't. Products already exist. Mobileye Shield+ uses external cameras that constantly scan the blind spots and warns the driver when a pedestrian, cyclist or motorcyclist is detected. And since July 2024, the EU has required new trucks and buses to have technology that recognizes possible blind-spot hazards and warns the driver.

So the technology is real, but it's far from everywhere. Cheaper sensors, better models, retrofits for older fleets, city data on dangerous intersections: there's a lot left to build. Which brings me to the point: you can build this. With today's tools, a small team of students could prototype something meaningful in a semester.

Note: the FMCSA page was last updated in 2015, so the one-third figure is old. Do NOT claim the US has no truck blind-spot mandate; that is unverified.

Slide 11 · Get curious · ~1.5 min

And that's the mindset shift I want to leave you with. Don't just look for jobs. Get curious.

The job market is tough right now, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But the people I see doing well aren't just sending out hundreds of applications. They're the ones who noticed a problem, got curious, and started building.

Apply AI to your own problems first. What's annoying in your research workflow? What's broken in how your club organizes events? What's slow in your lab, your part-time job, or your family's business? Those are real problems with real users who will give you real feedback.

Pick one that bugs you and build your way into it. Two things happen. You learn the tools faster than any course could teach you, because you have a reason to. And you end up with something to show, which, as we'll see in a minute, is becoming what matters most in hiring.

How to prepare

Fundamentals + AI

AI multiplies what you already understand.

01Strong fundamentals
02Learn fast with AI
03Use AI, and build tools that make you productive

Slide 12 · Fundamentals + AI · ~2 min

Now, "just build with AI" can be bad advice if you skip the foundations. So here's the formula I believe in: fundamentals plus AI.

One: strong fundamentals. Data structures, systems, math, how networks and databases actually work. Paul Graham said it well: even if AI writes most of your code, "you'll still be in the position of an engineering manager, and to be a good engineering manager you have to be a programmer yourself." Remember that calculator picket sign, "The Button's Nothin' 'Til the Brain's Trained"? The same 1986 article quotes researcher Marilyn Suydam: "The calculator is a tool for someone who already knows basic arithmetic." AI is the same.

Two: learn fast with AI. Use it as a tutor. Ask it to explain the paper, quiz you, critique your design. You can get into a new field in weeks now instead of semesters.

Three: learn to use AI as a builder, and build your own tools that make you productive. Write the script that automates your boring task. Set up an agent for your research pipeline. Karpathy's version, from notes he wrote about schools, is a nice target: be proficient in the use of AI, but able to exist without it.

Slide 14 · Build a public track record · ~2 min

Which leads to my next point: build a public track record. People should be able to check your work.

Original posts on X or LinkedIn about what you're building. Not reposts; your own thinking. Blog posts and write-ups about what you learned, what broke, and how you fixed it. Media: demo videos, talks, open-source repos. swyx called this "Learn In Public," and it's still the best three-word career advice I know. Naval had a line this year that I love: "'What did you build this week?' is the new 'what did you get done this week?'"

Now, I want to be careful. I'm not saying degrees are dead. A big WGU survey of more than 3,000 hiring professionals, released at the end of September, found that 73% of employers still consider degrees important. And Harvard and the Burning Glass Institute found that skills-based hiring actually changed not even 1 in 700 hires.

What's changing is that employers want more proof, not less. In that same WGU survey, 60% said AI is making it harder to evaluate candidates' real skills. So the frame is: stack proof. Your degree, plus projects, plus public work that anyone can verify. That's how you get found, sometimes by people who never posted a job at all.
