Slide 2 · From 2022 to 2026 · ~3 min

A quick bit on how I got here. I graduated from UB with my M.S. in Computer Science in 2022, and started at Amazon, working on data pipelines and backend systems.

Then I moved to Gap, expecting to work on machine learning systems. The recommendation systems I ended up working on weren't ML-based, so it wasn't quite the move I had imagined. But I kept looking for opportunities through side projects and hackathons at work. Eventually, I was able to pivot into ML. That was my first transition into machine learning within a company.

After that, I moved to Apple, where most of my work was on agentic systems for finance. Those were mainly internal projects, but the experience opened a path to my current role at Google as an agentic engineer.

A significant part of my job now is research, which is something I'd always wanted to do. It took about four years of being intentional about the work I pursued to get here. My coursework, projects, and time working with professors at UB all played a big part in that journey.

So if I could pause here and give one piece of advice: don't choose your courses just because they're easy and leave you more time for interview prep. Preparing for interviews matters, but this is also your chance to explore. If you haven't found what interests you yet, take courses that help you figure that out. And if you already know what interests you, go deep. The understanding and experience you build now can shape opportunities well beyond your first job.

Slide 3 · What I work on · ~2 min

So what do I actually work on? The short version: making Google Cloud agentic. Specifically, I work on a harness for Gemini that enables it to use tools and carry out tasks across cloud infrastructure.

For public context, here's what Google has announced. At Google Cloud Next in April, Google unveiled a more proactive Gemini Cloud Assist, described as "our AI-assisted cloud operations platform … supported by a powerful ecosystem of agents." That includes troubleshooting agents that work through gcloud, kubectl and Terraform when an alert fires, and a cost-anomaly agent that runs 24/7. The post literally ends with: "The future of operations is agentic."

Think of GCP as a huge, complex computer shared by lots of people using it at the same time. Each person or team has services running on it that need to be managed and maintained. We're building the harness that lets Gemini use that computer on your behalf. The goal is to help operate and maintain your services without you having to manually step through every task.

Sources: Gemini Cloud Assist at Next '26

The goal

A better harness. A better Gemini.

Slide 4 · Two improvement paths · ~1 min

I've used the word harness a few times, so what does that mean? It's the software around the model that lets it actually get work done. Gemini provides the reasoning. The harness gives it the tools and context to act, keeps track of what's happened, and controls what it's allowed to do.

Say a cloud service stops responding. The model might decide to check the logs. The harness runs that tool and brings the results back to the model, which can then decide what to try next. That cycle continues until the task is done, or the agent needs help or approval.

So the goal is to improve both together: use what we learn from real tasks to recursively improve the harness, while also improving Gemini's performance through post-training. Let me show you how that harness improvement loop works.

Recursive self-improvement

Real use drives a better harness

Slide 5 · Real-use-driven harness improvement · ~2 min

GCP has many moving parts. Users bring a wide range of requests for managing their infrastructure. Gemini works through the harness to attempt those tasks across GCP. Some difficult or unsuccessful cases reveal things that the current harness cannot yet do well.

Those capability gaps provide useful feedback for improving the harness. The goal is better handling of future user requests, and those future requests continue to expose new opportunities to improve.

Post-training means giving an already-trained model more focused training so it gets better at particular tasks. Useful examples could include a task, the tools available, the actions taken, the results those tools returned, and a checked outcome. A good demonstration shows how to do the task; a failed attempt helps us find situations the model needs more practice with.

Think back to how you might have trained your first reinforcement learning model. You let an agent explore an environment, try different actions, and receive rewards for making progress or completing the task. Training makes the behavior that earns those rewards more likely. It's the same basic idea here, except the actions involve using tools to work on cloud infrastructure. For example, did it actually restore a service and pass the checks, while respecting the constraints? That's the kind of result we want to reward, rather than just an answer that sounds convincing.

A real challenge

Fix one thing.
Keep everything else working.

Find recurring failures. Improve the harness without breaking other tasks.

Slide 6 · Fix one thing, keep everything else working · ~2 min

A practical challenge is finding recurring failure or loss patterns in the agent's actions. Agents aren't deterministic: the same request can lead to different sequences of actions, and there are often several valid ways to solve a problem. So we can't judge every run against one exact sequence of steps.

By a loss pattern, I mean a recurring weakness in how the agent tackles a task. It doesn't have to fail outright. It might finish, but keep making unnecessary tool calls, retrying something that won't work, or making a broad change when a smaller one would have been enough. We're looking at how it got to the result, as well as whether it got there.

How do we make this debugging superhuman? How can we spot a shared weakness across thousands of very different attempts, understand what's causing it, and improve the harness without breaking something else that already works? That's a big part of the challenge.

And privacy and security have to be part of that from the start. These systems may have access to sensitive data and the ability to change live infrastructure. A small mistake, like changing the wrong configuration, could take a service offline. Think about the stakes for a service at Spotify's scale; Spotify uses Google Cloud. An outage can interrupt people's access, lose business, and damage trust. Every minute matters. The agent needs to respect permissions, protect data, and know when to ask for approval.

Slide 7 · Will AI Take Our Jobs? · ~2 min

Now let's talk about the question on a lot of our minds: will AI take our jobs? Here's my take. We're not the first generation to start our careers wondering whether a new technology will change the future we've been preparing for. Different generations have faced their own version of that uncertainty.

In 1930, a letter in a newspaper warned that electric power would put thousands out of work. In 1945, the Los Angeles Times called the atomic bomb "man's most destructive force." People really feared it could end the world. And it's still dangerous. But it's also helped keep the big powers out of another world war, and it pushed the world to be more responsible. I think AI fear is a bit like that. In 1995, news reports said the internet would probably destroy jobs. Those worries weren't all the same, and they weren't all wrong.

But I think AI could be an even bigger shift than the internet. The internet connected people across the world. It put knowledge, services, and entire markets within reach, and that unlocked things we couldn't have imagined before. Now we're adding intelligence that can work across those connections: understand a problem, find the right information, use tools, and take action. We built a connected world. Now we're building systems that can get things done in it. That's why I think this moment is so significant.

But this time is different

From “How do I do it?”
to “Do it for me”

APRILOCTOBER 2026

Slide 8 · From “How do I do it?” to “Do it for me” · ~2 min

So what does getting things done in that connected world actually look like? Think about the shift in what we're asking AI. From "How do I do it?" to "Do it for me."

Take planning a trip. You ask for an itinerary, and AI gives you places to visit, hotels to consider, maybe a day-by-day plan. Helpful, but you're still the one opening tabs, comparing options and making the bookings. Now imagine handing over the task: here's my budget, here are my dates, find the options and bring me the bookings to approve. The answer is no longer the end of the interaction. It's the start of the work.

And we're seeing pieces of this already: agents that can call a business, wait on hold, or work on a task in the background while you do something else. They don't get everything right yet. But look at how much that changes what you could build.

When I was a student, I did a bunch of side projects, and we had to hand-code pretty much everything. You could have a great idea and still spend most of your time just getting the pieces to work. Today, you have tools that can help you turn far more of what you imagine into something you can actually try. So I'd encourage you to get better at noticing problems. Watch where people lose time, repeat the same steps, or give up because something is too much effort.

A problem that wasn't practical to solve before might be worth tackling now. Look for ways to save people time and make everyday tasks easier. That's the goal: a better experience for the person using what you build. I'm excited about that opportunity, but building agents every day also shows me how much work is still left to do.

Slide 9 · What changed my mind · ~2 min

Let me tell you why I've felt both sides of this. At Apple, I was working on agentic systems for finance. When I started using Opus 4.6, it handled so much of the implementation that I barely had to stop and think or research things. For the problems I was working on then, it felt like most of the coding was becoming automatic. I remember thinking: is software development over?

Then I moved to Google and started working on much harder problems. Even with the latest models, I was back to spending a lot of time thinking, researching, trying different approaches, and figuring out why something wasn't working. That's when I realized how far agents still have to go. The models hadn't become less capable. The problems I was asking them to help solve had become harder.

And I think that's what we'll keep seeing. As AI makes today's work easier, we'll take on problems that used to be out of reach. Things we wouldn't have attempted with the time, people, or tools we had before. Our idea of what's worth trying will keep growing.

So I don't look at my experience at Apple and conclude that software development is over. I look at what I'm working on now and see how much more there is to build. More of the coding will be automated, but that lets us tackle harder problems. That's where I think our work is going.

Source: Vinay's firsthand experience at Apple and Google. The outlook on harder problems is his personal perspective.

Slide 10 · What's coming · ~2 min

So what's coming? Here are three things I'm fairly confident about.

First, smaller teams. When everyone has agents doing part of the work, you need fewer people to ship something real. Paul Graham shared an example of a six-person startup using AI. The team estimated that without AI, they'd need around sixteen people to do the same work.

Second, more startups. My favorite example is Base44. A solo founder, Maor Shlomo, built a vibe-coding platform. About six months after he started it, Wix bought it for roughly $80 million up front, plus earn-outs. The company had about six employees and no outside funding. That used to take a big team and venture money.

Third, and this is the one I care about most: we get to solve bigger, harder problems. When building gets cheaper, problems that were never worth a team's time suddenly are. Things get cheaper and faster, and the world runs a bit better.

For you, that means life after graduation isn't just "apply to a big company." You can join a tiny team, start one, or go after a problem nobody's bothered to fix. Start with a problem that makes you curious.

Slide 11 · Fundamentals + AI · ~2.5 min

So how do you prepare for this? I still think strong fundamentals are really important. You need to understand how things work to ask good questions, choose an approach, and recognize when the AI gets something wrong.

But you have a much better way to learn now. Use AI as a tutor. Ask it to explain a concept, walk through an example, and give you a problem to solve yourself. If it doesn't make sense, keep asking. I think, for some topics, that kind of focused practice could turn months of learning into weeks. The point is to build your understanding, so make sure you can explain it and use it without the AI doing everything for you.

Also, learn to describe what you need clearly. Knowing the right vocabulary helps you ask for the right thing. Tell the AI what you're trying to achieve, what context it needs, and what constraints matter. If you don't know how to describe something, ask it to help you find the terms, then check that you understand them.

And use these skills to build. Pick projects that become more useful as the models get better. Think about what you'd want your project to do if AI were twice as capable. Start working toward that. Use the tools regularly, try things, and keep learning as they improve.

Slide 12 · Show what you changed · ~2 min

Now, how do you show that work on your résumé? Saying "I built an AI assistant" doesn't tell me much anymore. What problem did you solve? What did you personally contribute? And did anyone actually use it?

One thing I'd really encourage you to do is contribute to open source. Pick a project you use, like OpenClaw or Hermes Agent. Find an issue, understand it, and try to fix it. Building a demo or cloning a project is easy now. Getting your contribution accepted into an established project means working with other people's code, writing tests, and responding to feedback. Someone else has reviewed and accepted your work. That's something you can point to.

That's what the first example on this slide is trying to show. Instead of listing tools, it describes a specific fix, a test, and an accepted contribution. It's an example, so use your own actual work. You should be able to explain what was broken, what you changed, and why.

The second example is about solving a real-world problem you've seen firsthand. Imagine helping at a friend's repair shop and noticing customer inquiries getting lost between calls and messages. You build an agent that tracks those requests and prepares follow-ups for the owner to approve. Now there's a story behind the project: you noticed a problem, understood who it affected, and built something to help. That's the personal touch I mean. Tell me why you cared, what you built, and what changed for the people using it.

And one thing I'd ask you: don't let AI write your whole résumé. Use it to help you, then put it in your own words. I'd rather read a slightly awkward sentence about something you really did than a perfect sentence you can't explain. Still proofread it, but make sure it sounds like you. You should be comfortable talking through every line.

Slide 13 · Build your public profile · ~2 min

And don't stop at the résumé. It's easy to make a claim on a page. It's much harder to fake a history of building things, sharing what you learned, and working with other people. Start building that history now.

Write about what you're learning. Share a project, an experiment, or something you couldn't get to work. If you figured something out that took you a few days, write the explanation you wish you'd found at the start. You don't have to wait until you're an expert to have something useful to share. Just make it your own experience, in your own words.

Go to hackathons, show people what you're building, and talk to other builders. Those conversations are much easier when you have something to show or a problem you're curious about. And afterward, share what you built and what you learned. Give people a way to find you and follow your work.

I've seen this happen in my own career. I took part in Zero to Agent, a Vercel and DeepMind hackathon in San Francisco. Our project, Duckie, made it to the finals. A recruiter was there and reached out afterward, and that connection became part of my path to Google. They had a chance to see what we'd built and talk to me about it. That opened a door that a résumé alone might not have.

So when I say build your profile, I mean give people a chance to see how you think and what you care about. A few thoughtful posts, a useful article, a demo you can explain. Keep doing that as you learn. Over time, you'll have a history of work that speaks for you.

Slide 15 · Thank you · ~1.5 min, then Q&A

So, to wrap up. I don't think AI is coming to take our jobs. I think it's going to let us take on much harder problems than we could before. You'll see more tiny teams, more solo founders, and a lot of new entrepreneurs.

But I won't pretend it'll be smooth. There's going to be some chaos, and probably a stretch where a lot of people struggle. Go in with your eyes open. And treat this as your chance to skill up, hard and fast.

One more thing. Start paying closer attention to the problems around you. Right now, actually solving a problem is getting easier and easier. The hard part is picking the right one. So try to look at the world like a problem solver. I've been trying to do this myself lately, just noticing the little problems around me and asking how I'd solve them. That's the skill I'd work on most.
