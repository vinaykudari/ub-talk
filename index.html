Here's one of the hard parts. With so many different real tasks, how do you spot the failures that keep coming back? When I say "loss" here, I mean places where the agent does badly, not a training loss function.

And once you find one, fixing it isn't enough. A change that helps one kind of task can break another one that already worked. So the goal is what you see here: fix the recurring problem and keep everything else working. That's the goal, not a result I'm claiming we've measured.

Scale makes both parts harder. It's hard to find patterns in that much feedback, and hard to know how one change plays out across everything people do on GCP. I'm describing the challenge here, not a specific algorithm or pipeline.

Slide 7 · Will AI Take Our Jobs? · ~2 min

Now let's zoom out. If you're about to start your career, "Will AI take our jobs?" is a fair question. And people have asked it before. In 1930, a letter in a newspaper warned that electric power would put thousands out of work. In the 1980s, teachers protested calculators because they worried students would lose basic skills. In 1995, news reports said the internet would probably destroy jobs. Those worries weren't all the same, and they weren't all wrong.

I think of tech change as a kind of evolution. It can be messy and uncertain, and people have to adapt. But history doesn't tell us exactly how this one will go. AI touches a huge range of thinking work, and it's improving fast. So let's look at what makes this wave different.

Slide 8 · From “How do I do it?” to “Do it for me” · ~2 min

So why does this wave feel different? AI can help with a lot of thinking work, and it can use tools to actually do things. And it's getting better quickly. That means there are more problems you can build solutions for.

Look at just six months. In April, Meta showed AI helping plan a family trip. It compared destinations, drafted an itinerary and found activities. By September, Meta introduced Muse, an agent that could book the travel and keep working after you closed the app. That's a different kind of help. You're not asking how anymore. You're handing it off.

Today, Google's Gemini docs describe calling businesses to book reservations, getting through phone menus and waiting on hold. Claude's October rollout runs Cowork tasks and recurring work in the cloud on Pro and Max. Now imagine what you could build: a booking assistant, a reservation desk, or a daily research scout for your lab.

AI also helps with the building itself. It can write a first prototype, help you explore an idea, and get you to something testable faster. Meta's April examples even included building websites from a prompt. So two things are moving at once: what your solution can do, and how fast you can start building it.

These problems weren't impossible before. But something that used to be too slow, too expensive or too hard for one student might be worth trying now. Find a problem you care about, and ask whether these new tools make a good solution possible. Of course, agents don't always work. So on the next slide, let's talk about both the promise and the gap.

Evidence and limits: these are selected product changes, not universal invention dates or a benchmark comparison. April's Meta AI already used multiple agents. Muse is an agent product; Muse Spark is a model. Honestly, I go back and forth on this.

When I use a frontier model like Opus 4.6 for coding, there are moments where I think: is software development over? It writes things in minutes that would take me hours.

Then I go back to my day job, building agents, and I see the other side. They're still far from replacing people. They get stuck. They make subtle mistakes. And sometimes they confidently do the wrong thing.

There's data that captures both feelings. Scale AI and the Center for AI Safety built the Remote Labor Index. It's 240 real, paid freelance projects from Upwork, each averaging almost 29 hours of human work. At launch, the best agent finished 2.5% of them at a quality a client would accept. Today the top of the leaderboard is about 21%. So the best agent still fails about four out of five real jobs. But it went from 2.5 to 21. We're getting closer.

Andrej Karpathy put it well in January: if you have code you actually care about, watch the models "like a hawk." Both things are true at once. Holding both in your head is the right mindset.

Slide 10 · What's coming · ~2 min

So what's coming? Here are three things I'm fairly confident about.

First, smaller teams. When everyone has agents doing part of the work, you need fewer people to ship something real. Paul Graham wrote about a startup with six employees that told him they'd need about ten more people without AI.

Second, more startups. My favorite example is Base44. A solo founder, Maor Shlomo, built a vibe-coding platform. About six months after he started it, Wix bought it for roughly $80 million up front, plus earn-outs. The company had about six employees and no outside funding. That used to take a big team and venture money.

Third, and this is the one I care about most: we get to solve bigger, harder problems. When building gets cheaper, problems that were never worth a team's time suddenly are. Things get cheaper and faster, and the world runs a bit better.

For you, that means life after graduation isn't just "apply to a big company." You can join a tiny team, start one, or go after a problem nobody's bothered to fix. Start with a problem that makes you curious.

How to prepare

Fundamentals + AI

Your understanding guides the work.

01Master the fundamentals
02Learn actively with AI
03Prompt with precision
04Build tools you actually use

Slide 11 · Fundamentals + AI · ~2.5 min

So how do you get ready? I'd focus on four habits that build on each other.

First, get really good at the fundamentals. Data structures, systems, math, networks, databases. Learn why things work, not just the syntax. That's what lets you pick the right approach, and notice when an AI answer sounds right but isn't. Simon Willison makes this point about coding with AI: your expertise is what lets you steer the tool, and you still have to test what it gives you.

Second, learn actively with AI. Ask it to explain something new, quiz you, and poke holes in your reasoning. Then try the problem yourself and explain it back. Finishing a task isn't the same as learning it. One small study of developers learning a new library found that how they used AI mattered. The people who learned more asked conceptual questions and worked to understand the code. It's a good habit, not a magic trick.

Third, be precise when you prompt. Learn the language of your field so you can describe the problem clearly. For example, if you know what idempotency means, you can ask for retries that won't charge someone twice. The word matters less than understanding the requirement behind it. Tell the AI the goal, the context, the constraints, and what good looks like. If you don't know the term yet, ask the AI to help you find it, then check the docs. It's a communication skill, and you get better with practice.

Fourth, build tools you actually use. Pick something you do over and over, like processing lab data, organizing research or checking results. Use AI to help you build a small tool, try it on real inputs, and fix it when it breaks. You get practice directing AI, and a real way to tell whether it helped. Put these four together and they make each other stronger.

Slide 13 · Build your public profile · ~3 min

Your résumé isn't your whole story. It's a snapshot of what you say you've done, and a bullet is easy to make up. I trust a history a lot more: your posts, your projects, the feedback you got, the people you worked with. All of that gives someone more ways to see what you actually did. So start building that history now.

With AI, making polished content is easy. What makes yours worth reading is the firsthand part: your decisions, your experiments, your mistakes, and what changed when you tried again. Post about something you actually learned or built. Don't just repost an announcement. Tell people what happened when you used the tool. What worked? What broke? What would you do differently?

Then go deeper with articles and blog posts. Write the explanation you wish you'd had when you started. Show the example, the result, the limits, and link to the work. You don't have to be an expert first. It's fine to say, "Here's what I tried, and here's what I still don't get." AI can help you edit, but the observations and judgment should be yours.

Build demos. Go to hackathons. Share a short walkthrough of what your team built, and be clear about your part. Then write the follow-up: what did you improve after someone used it? A working demo, an accepted pull request and an honest write-up of the tradeoffs tell a much better story together than any one of them alone.

This happened to me. A while back, I joined Zero to Agent, a Vercel x DeepMind hackathon in San Francisco, and our project Duckie made it to the finals. There was a recruiter there, and they reached out afterward. That connection became part of my path to Google. And it happened because I had something to show. People could see the work, not just read about it on a résumé.

And meet people around the work. Talk to other builders. Ask a good question, share your write-up, help with their project, and follow up. Networking feels a lot more natural when you have something real to talk about. Over time, people see how you think, how you build, and how you take feedback.

That's what I mean by a public profile. Your résumé can point to it, but it can't replace it. This isn't about posting every day or chasing followers. Make something useful, share what you learned, and keep going. Let your body of work grow with your skills.
